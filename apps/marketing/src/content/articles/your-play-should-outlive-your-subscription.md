A theatre gets in touch about a play you put aside four years ago. They like it, but the cast is smaller than you had in mind. Could two characters become one? And would you consider restoring the scene you cut before the first reading?

You remember the scene. Finding a version you can work on takes longer.

There is a PDF in your email, another on an old laptop, and an account with the writing service you used at the time. You need to work out which draft contains the cuts from the reading, whether you still have an editable copy, and where you put the earlier material.

Writing software is happy to sell you a subscription. It owes you a clear explanation of what happens when you stop paying. For a playwright, leaving a draft alone for a few years is an ordinary part of the work. Coming back to it should not require buying your way into the writing room again.

That is a useful situation to imagine when choosing writing software. The editor that feels comfortable today also has to leave you with something you can use when the play comes back.

## A play can outlast the tool it was written in

An early draft, a workshop script, and a published text may be separated by years. A musical adds more moving parts: dialogue changes, revised lyrics, new arrangements, and scores that need to match what the cast is rehearsing.

During those gaps, a laptop might be replaced. A subscription might lapse. A writing application might change its prices or stop being maintained.

Adobe Story is a concrete example. Adobe announced its closure in January 2018 and gave existing users until January 22, 2019 to download their work. Its offline application would also stop working after that date. Writers had a year's notice, but they still had to move their material to another system. [Adobe's announcement](https://blog.adobe.com/en/publish/2018/01/22/adobe-exits-scriptwriting-software-business-end-adobe-story-cc) remains a useful reminder to check what happens when a service ends.

"Your work is safe with us" is reassuring until you have to ask who "us" will be in five years. Companies can make sensible business decisions that leave writers with an unwanted migration job.

Before committing several years of drafts to an application, try taking one script out of it. Open the export somewhere else. See what you actually have.

## A PDF is only part of the archive

A PDF gives a director, actor, or reader a stable copy of the script. It preserves the pages you sent, which matters when rehearsal notes refer to a particular line on page 37.

Revising from that PDF can be awkward. Copying the text into another application may leave you repairing line breaks, character cues, or page headers. The words survived, but some of the work around them has to be done again.

An editable source file serves a different purpose. Ideally, it preserves enough structure for an editor to recognise characters, dialogue, stage directions, and scene divisions. A plain-text format also gives you a way to read the content without the original application, though another editor may interpret or display it differently.

For a musical, there is a further question: where are the scores? A script containing a reference to a PDF is not necessarily a backup of that PDF. The same applies to notes, research, and other attachments.

It helps to keep a dated reading copy, an editable source, and the supporting material together. Give a workshop draft a name you will understand later. If it uses a particular version of the score, keep that version with it. These are small jobs while a project is active; reconstructing them years later can take an afternoon.

## The awkward fit between a monthly bill and an unfinished play

Subscriptions can be reasonable. During an active collaboration, shared editing, comments, version history, and access from several devices may save enough time to justify the cost.

The arrangement becomes harder to defend if you have to renew a subscription just to make a few changes to an old draft. A business wants recurring revenue. A playwright may need three evenings with a script that has been untouched since the last workshop. Those schedules have very little in common.

As of October 2026, WriterDuet's free plan allows up to three projects and lets free users download their scripts without watermarks or page limits. A project cap may be inconvenient, but it does not mean the writer has lost access to the text. [WriterDuet FAQ](https://www.writerduet.com/article/4-writerduet-faq).

The questions worth asking are specific. Can you export an editable copy on the free plan? What happens to existing projects when a paid plan ends? Can you still download them? Does the export include everything you need?

Clear answers let you decide whether the service suits your working habits. Someone developing a show with three collaborators may make a different choice from someone returning alone to an unfinished play once a year.

But "you can always export a PDF" is a poor answer to someone asking how they will keep revising their play. A service that makes it easy to start writing should make it just as easy to leave with an editable copy. Writers are entitled to be demanding about that.

## Keeping a copy you can work with

Tools that keep the primary copy of your work on your device reduce your dependence on a remote account. That can suit the quiet stretches of writing between workshops and productions.

A browser application can work this way too. Running in a browser does not necessarily mean a document is stored on a company's server. It is worth checking where the application saves your work and how you retrieve it.

Local storage still needs a backup. Browser data can be cleared, and devices can be lost or fail. Downloading a copy and keeping it somewhere separate should be an ordinary part of finishing a writing session or setting a draft aside.

Readable formats help as well. [Fountain](https://fountain.io/syntax/), for example, represents a script in plain text and includes conventions for simultaneous dialogue and lyrics. Even without the original writing application, the text remains accessible. The formatting and production workflow depend on the software using it.

Open-source software offers another useful safeguard: its published code can be kept and, under its licence, maintained or adapted. That gives people options if the original developers move on.

The amount of work you have put into a script should never become the reason you feel unable to leave its editor.

Before settling on any tool, make a small test script. Include an awkward scene, overlapping dialogue, and a song if you are writing a musical. Export it, back it up, and restore it. You will learn more from that exercise than from a promise that your work is “future-proof.”

## Where Stagistic Editor fits

[Stagistic Editor](https://stagistic.com) brings these concerns into a writing tool built for theatre. It is free and open source, starts without an account, and stores scripts locally in your browser. It has dedicated blocks for acts, scenes, character cues, dialogue, stage directions, lyrics, and music cues. For musical writers, it can combine the script and attached score PDFs into one export with continuous page numbering.

You can download the script as a readable `.stagistic` text file using the documented [Stagistic Syntax](https://docs.stagistic.com/syntax/). The `.stagistic` text file does not include attached score PDFs. For a fuller backup, including metadata and assets, the project also provides the `.stepkg` package format. Those copies still need to be saved somewhere safe. The practical benefit is being able to use a visual editor designed for the work while keeping files you can take with you when you set the play aside—or pick it up again.
